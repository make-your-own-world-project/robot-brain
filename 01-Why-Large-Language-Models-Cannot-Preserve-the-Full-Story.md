# Why large language models cannot preserve the full story

![Saved pieces lose value when their sources, relationships, and history are separated.](illustrations/ordinary-storage-loses-context.png)

The strongest paid language models used while building this project could do impressive work. They could write, research, explain, and help solve difficult problems. They still could not preserve the full history behind a long project.

A later answer might remember the conclusion but lose the failed attempts, corrections, and evidence that led to it. Earlier instructions could disappear when a conversation became too long. The model would continue writing as though nothing important had been lost.

That is a serious problem when the missing history represents someone's time, knowledge, or experience.

## Files are not enough

A folder can hold every note, conversation, image, and task while still losing the story that connects them.

Months later, a person may need to know:

- what started the work
- which ideas were considered
- why one attempt failed
- what evidence changed the plan
- which conclusion is current
- what is still unknown
- why an old note matters now

Search can find a file with similar words. It cannot reliably answer those questions. Sending a larger pile of files to a language model does not create permanent memory either. The service sees what was selected for that request. When the request ends, the useful connections may disappear again.

## Training also loses the original setting

Language models learn patterns from enormous collections of human work. That is what makes them useful. It is also why they cannot act as a faithful archive of everything that shaped them.

Ideas from one book, article, conversation, translation, or community become mixed with ideas from many others. The model does not keep each work intact with its author, purpose, audience, evidence, disagreement, and later corrections attached.

The original work may still exist somewhere else. A provider may also keep separate copies. The loss described here happens inside the trained model: it keeps useful influence from the work but cannot rebuild the complete human meaning around it.

Repeating a sentence is not the same as preserving that meaning. A model may reproduce familiar words without knowing why they were written, what situation they described, whose view was missing, or what happened later.

## The missing history also hides bias

No language model learned from the whole world.

Its knowledge reflects what was written down, preserved, collected, translated, licensed, labeled, and selected. It also reflects what was missing. Some languages and communities have far more published material than others. Archives preserve the views of powerful institutions more often than private, local, or oral knowledge.

People building the model make more choices about what to remove, reward, discourage, or treat as a good answer. Product rules add another layer. A finished response can carry all of those influences without showing which one affected a particular sentence.

A citation found during a new request does not reveal this full history. It shows a source used or named for that request, not everything that taught the model how to interpret the subject.

## What this project keeps instead

Robot Brain keeps the source before asking any model for help interpreting it. The source does not change when a summary, correction, or new interpretation is added.

Later work is saved beside it with a date and a link back to the relevant passage. A failed attempt can remain visible. A corrected conclusion can point to the evidence that changed it. If the reason for a change is unknown, the record says so.

When someone needs an answer or document, the request builder gathers the part of that history needed for the job. The result can be shorter than the full record without pretending to replace it.

A language model may help with that result. It cannot erase the sources, rewrite the past, or make an unsupported guess become part of the accepted record.

## The practical test

A useful result should let a reader answer four questions:

1. What happened?
2. What evidence supports this account?
3. What changed, failed, or remains disputed?
4. What is still unknown?

If the record cannot answer one of those questions, polished language should not hide the gap.
