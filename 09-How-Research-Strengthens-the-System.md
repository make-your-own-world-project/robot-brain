# Why the design draws from many fields

![Different fields contribute limited questions without being blended into one authority.](illustrations/context-scientific-disciplines-hero.png)

No single field can answer every question raised by a person's history.

Research on archives and changing information helps preserve where something came from. Work on arguments helps separate a claim from its support. Language research helps explain how documents are organized. Studies of how people use tools help protect the reader's time. Testing practices help keep a confident model response from being accepted without evidence.

Each field contributes a limited method. None becomes the final authority over a person.

## Why one language model is not enough

A language model blends patterns from scientific writing, everyday speech, translations, historical records, product rules, and unsupported repetition. That broad mix can be useful, but the answer does not show which influence supplied each idea.

This project keeps different questions visible:

- Where did this material come from, and how did it change?
- What claim is being made?
- What supports or disputes it?
- Which passages may be relevant?
- What must the finished document explain?
- How much work will the reader need to understand it?
- What did the test actually establish?

Different focused readers can help answer those questions. Their results stay linked to the methods and sources behind them.

## Credit records how an idea was involved

Outside work may have been used directly, adapted, tested, compared, rejected, or simply studied. Those are different relationships, and the credit should say which one occurred.

Studying an idea does not transfer its rights. Using a public computer tool does not make its authors responsible for this project. Work returned by a commercial language model is recorded with the model, date, request, and material it received. It is not treated as anonymous project knowledge.

## Published knowledge has gaps

Some communities have more books, archives, translations, and online material than others. Oral, private, local, and less widely translated knowledge may be missing. Historical collections can also preserve the views of powerful institutions more often than the people affected by them.

Smooth writing should not hide those gaps. Sources and conflicting interpretations remain identifiable. Missing viewpoints can be recorded without pretending to describe them.
